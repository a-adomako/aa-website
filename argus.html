The breaking point

At Augmentum, an influencer marketing agency, we manage campaigns involving thousands of creators producing videos, photos, Stories, and other social content.

Tracking that content is essential. Campaign managers need to know how much has been produced, who produced it, which campaign it belongs to, and whether it's ready to be delivered to clients.

We already had an automated system handling much of this work.

It was built around Google Drive, Google Sheets, and Make. But as the business grew, the system was becoming increasingly difficult to maintain.

Then, one weekend, a relatively minor change exposed how fragile the infrastructure had become.

One of our content tracking platforms had slightly changed its export filename convention.

Our system relied on those filenames to identify creators and determine where their content belonged.

When the naming convention changed, the assumptions behind our processing logic broke.

Suddenly, the content numbers the team relied on across more than 18 campaigns could no longer be trusted.

The team needed answers. How much content had actually been produced? Which creators had delivered? Which files were duplicates? What were the correct numbers?

They turned to me.

I could have updated the formulas and patched the Make scenarios. But that would have addressed the immediate symptom without solving the underlying problem.

The system was already bursting at the seams.

I decided to rebuild the infrastructure.

Understanding the original system

The previous process consisted of several interconnected workflows.

When content was uploaded to Google Drive, a Make automation detected the new files and recorded their names, IDs, and timestamps in Google Sheets.

Spreadsheet formulas then extracted usernames and dates from the filenames.

Other formulas matched those usernames to the campaign's creator list to determine their identities and stages.

A second Make automation inspected the resulting rows, searched Google Drive for the corresponding files, and routed them into the appropriate folders.

Content might be categorized as Affiliate, Pending Usage, or Unmatched, sometimes with additional geographic routing.

The result was an automated workflow, but one with important weaknesses.

Filenames had become part of the business's data infrastructure. If their format changed, creator matching could fail.

The logic was distributed across multiple systems. Google Sheets handled interpretation and matching, Make handled routing, and Google Drive stored the files.

Every new client introduced additional maintenance. Client-specific folders, spreadsheet configurations, and automation branches made the system progressively more complex.

Worse, a failed lookup could appear to mean that a creator didn't exist, rather than that something had gone wrong with the system.

The reporting incident showed why this mattered.

When a business relies on a system to measure its performance, silent processing errors can be more damaging than visible failures.

What I built: Argus

I designed and built Argus, a centralized content operations platform that brings ingestion, creator identification, deduplication, classification, storage, review, and delivery into one application.

I was responsible for the entire project, from the initial architecture to production deployment.

I developed it using AI coding tools, while owning the product design, technical decisions, integrations, database, user interface, and implementation.

1. A centralized processing engine

Instead of spreading the processing logic across spreadsheet formulas and automation scenarios, I moved it into a purpose-built application.

Argus accepts content uploads, including large ZIP archives, and processes files in the background.

It extracts relevant metadata, identifies creators, checks campaign membership, detects duplicates, and determines what should happen to each file.

The system supports multiple export naming conventions, making it less dependent on a single external platform's formatting decisions.

Files that cannot be interpreted reliably are treated as exceptions rather than silently assigned an incorrect identity.

2. Automatic content classification

This is one of the capabilities I'm most proud of.

For every incoming file, Argus uses structured rules and creator data to answer several questions:

  • Who created this content?
  • Is the creator associated with the campaign?
  • What stage is the creator currently in?
  • Has this content already been received?
  • Should it be delivered, excluded, grouped, or reviewed?

Argus handles these decisions automatically whenever the available information is sufficient.

It also understands important differences between content formats.

For example, several images belonging to a carousel can represent one post, while multiple Stories published on the same day remain separate pieces of content.

Rather than applying the same assumptions to every file, the system processes content according to explicit rules.

In its first eight weeks, approximately 98% of files required no individual human action.

3. Reliable duplicate detection

The original system depended heavily on filenames and Google Drive searches.

But two files can contain exactly the same video while having different names.

I introduced SHA-256 file hashing to identify exact duplicates using the actual file contents.

This allows Argus to recognize repeated uploads regardless of their filenames.

During the first eight weeks, it automatically caught 20,137 exact duplicates, representing approximately 88.5 GB of data.

This provides a more reliable basis for counting unique content than relying on filenames alone.

4. A database built for scale

Google Sheets was previously responsible for substantial creator lookup and classification work.

I replaced that processing layer with an indexed database that can handle large creator lists and frequent synchronization.

Argus now tracks more than 609,000 creator accounts across its connected client catalogue.

The agency's existing creator spreadsheets remain part of the workflow, but Argus synchronizes their information and handles the processing internally.

A particularly important design decision was preserving the last known-good data whenever a synchronization fails.

If an import is incomplete or a lookup encounters an error, Argus doesn't simply replace valid data with an incomplete result.

It records the problem and preserves reliable information until the issue can be resolved.

This makes failures visible and recoverable rather than allowing them to silently distort the operation.

5. Human review for exceptions

I designed the system to automate predictable decisions without pretending that every situation can be resolved automatically.

When Argus encounters genuinely uncertain content, it places it in a dedicated review queue.

The interface provides suggestions and streamlined controls so a team member can resolve the issue quickly.

Some decisions, such as correctly linking a creator's account, can resolve several files at once.

This approach allows humans to focus on exceptions instead of repeatedly processing files that follow known rules.

6. A product people can actually use

The interface was a major part of the project.

I didn't want the team to replace spreadsheet complexity with an equally complicated application.

Argus needed to make powerful capabilities accessible without requiring users to understand the underlying processing architecture.

I built a visual content library where team members can browse, search, filter, preview, organize, and download content.

I also built interfaces for creator records, classification reviews, affiliate tracking, sales data, system activity, and client-specific access.

Instead of navigating multiple Drive folders and spreadsheets, users can work inside one application.

7. A foundation for more valuable data

One of my favorite outcomes is that Argus makes our existing data far more useful.

Previously, much of the information was trapped in spreadsheets, folder structures, and disconnected automations.

Now, creator identities, campaign stages, content records, processing decisions, and other operational information can be connected through a structured database.

That opens the door to richer reporting, creator performance analysis, integrations with other internal tools, and additional automation.

Argus began as a solution to a content operations problem.

But the architecture creates opportunities to build capabilities beyond the original requirements.

Rebuilding trust in the numbers

The immediate problem that triggered Argus was unreliable campaign reporting.

Running the content through Argus gave us a consistent way to identify creators, exclude irrelevant files, recognize exact duplicates, and calculate which pieces should count toward campaign content totals.

The team could examine the processed content rather than relying entirely on the outputs of fragmented automations.

In practice, this gave us the most dependable content picture we'd had from the operation.

Just as importantly, it simplified reporting going forward.

The system records how files were processed and allows people to investigate the underlying information when something appears wrong.

Instead of treating reported totals as unexplained numbers produced by spreadsheets, the team has a more traceable foundation for its content records.

The measurable results

67% less daily handling time

Previously, one team member spent approximately 4.5 hours a day collecting, checking, organizing, and delivering content.

After Argus, that person estimated the work took approximately 1.5 hours.

That represents an estimated reduction of three hours per day, or 15 hours per five-day working week.

The estimate excludes additional time previously spent by other team members finding and correcting mistakes.

More than 122,000 files processed

In its first eight weeks, Argus processed 122,440 files totaling 657 GB.

Of those, 58,327 unique content items were delivered to client libraries.

It automatically detected 20,137 exact duplicate files and filtered out 42,314 irrelevant or out-of-scope files.

Routine decisions largely automated

Only 2,227 files had a recorded individual human action.

By November 9, just 261 files—0.21% of all files processed—remained in the review queue.

One platform across multiple campaigns

Argus processed content across 26 client libraries during its first eight weeks.

Rather than building new bespoke automations for each client, the team can configure clients inside the shared application.

The same processing logic can then be applied consistently across campaigns.

Engineering for reliability

Building Argus required solving problems that only became visible at production scale.

Large creator lists could time out during synchronization. Concurrent jobs could interfere with each other. Malformed filenames could cause unexpected parsing errors.

One import even failed because a creator's name was truncated in the middle of an emoji.

I addressed these issues through better error handling, safer text processing, paginated data transfers, job coordination, and safeguards against incomplete updates.

I also developed automated tests around critical system behavior.

By November 9, the platform had 395 passing unit and database tests.

The biggest lesson was that reliable software needs to handle failure deliberately.

A system shouldn't quietly invent certainty when the underlying data is missing or a dependency has failed.

The takeaway

The incident that started Argus looked like a broken automation.

A third-party platform had changed its filename format, and our reporting could no longer be trusted.

The obvious solution was to repair the formulas and move on.

But that would have preserved the same weaknesses.

I chose to rebuild the system around structured data, centralized business logic, automatic classification, and explicit handling of failures.

Argus turned an operational reporting problem into an opportunity to build more reliable infrastructure—and significantly reduce the work required to run it.

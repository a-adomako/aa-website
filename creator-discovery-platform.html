The problem

At Augmentum, an influencer marketing agency, discovering creators for client campaigns was a highly manual process.

Each client had separate Google Sheets for creator discovery and rejected profiles. Team members searched for potential influencers, entered their information, and passed them to another team for vetting.

Campaign managers relied on manual updates to understand progress. Team capacity was tracked separately, and productivity estimates were largely based on assumptions rather than actual work.

There was another expensive problem: finding creator email addresses.

Our previous enrichment workflow cost approximately $2,000 per month through Clay for around 25,000 emails.

As the agency scaled, we needed a better way to discover, evaluate, organize, and deliver creators without continuously increasing manual work or paying repeatedly for information we already had.

What I built

I designed and built the Augmentum Discovery Platform (ADP), a full-stack web application that brought the creator discovery operation into one system.

I built the entire product from start to finish, using AI to accelerate development while making the architectural, product, and implementation decisions myself.

ADP wasn't just a replacement for spreadsheets. It redesigned how information and work moved through the agency.

1. One shared creator database

Instead of storing creator information separately for every client, I built a centralized database that maintains a consistent identity for each creator.

This allows the agency to reuse previously discovered creators across multiple campaigns rather than starting from scratch each time.

The database now contains over 291,000 unique creators, with more than 147,000 appearing across multiple campaigns.

2. A structured vetting workflow

I replaced spreadsheet-based vetting with a dedicated interface where team members can review creators, approve or reject them, and record why.

Every decision is timestamped and traceable.

The platform also supports rejection challenges, corrections, and additional quality reviews, making the process more accountable and easier to analyze.

In its first 139 days, ADP recorded more than 208,000 human vetting decisions.

3. Intelligent workload allocation

Previously, team capacity and assignments were managed through a separate process using manually entered productivity estimates.

I built workload management into ADP itself.

The platform measures individual work activity, calculates available capacity, and uses AI to recommend how tasks should be distributed.

Managers retain control over approvals, while the system handles much of the calculation and coordination.

4. Automated creator email enrichment

I replaced our previous Clay-based email discovery workflow with an enrichment pipeline integrated directly into ADP.

The process prioritizes information we already own:

  1. Search our internal database for an existing email address.
  2. If unavailable, query an external creator data provider.
  3. Use Apify-based retrieval as an additional fallback.
  4. Verify retrieved addresses and store the results for future reuse.

This turned creator contact information into a growing internal asset instead of treating every email discovery as a new purchase.

The system accumulated 182,028 email addresses, of which 151,495 were verified as deliverable.

Approximately 70% of those addresses came from our own database, reducing the need for fresh external lookups.

5. Real-time campaign visibility

I built dedicated interfaces for campaign managers to request creators, monitor progress, view available profiles, and export results.

Instead of waiting for someone to update a spreadsheet or send a report, managers can access operational information directly.

The platform also preserves campaign briefs, historical changes, assignments, and vetting decisions.

The hardest part: Making complexity feel simple

The most difficult challenge wasn't writing the code.

It was designing a system that an entire team could use without needing lengthy explanations.

ADP had to support different people doing different jobs: creator discovery, vetting, campaign management, and administration.

Each role needed the right information and controls without being overwhelmed by everything the system could do.

I approached this as a product design problem as much as an engineering problem.

The goal was to make complex workflows feel natural to the people performing them, while keeping the underlying data, automation, and rules consistent.

Building the system with AI accelerated implementation, but deciding how it should work—and especially how people should interact with it—remained the central challenge.

The results

In its first 139 days, ADP supported 26 client campaigns and handed over more than 251,000 creator profiles.

A major contributor was the ability to reuse creators across campaigns.

In the historical spreadsheet data, campaigns averaged approximately 2,585 profiles per campaign-month. During ADP's July–October period, that figure was approximately 3,501—an indicative increase of about 35% in profiles handed over per campaign-month.

The comparison is not a controlled productivity measurement, but it illustrates the value of a shared creator database.

Other outcomes included:

  • 208,161 recorded human vetting decisions, providing an auditable history of review activity.
  • 151,495 verified email addresses accumulated through the enrichment pipeline.
  • 53% of handed-over profiles reused prior approval from another campaign without requiring another human vetting pass on the receiving campaign.
  • 34 active user accounts across discovery, campaign management, and administrative roles.
  • A complete replacement of the previous Clay enrichment workflow, with existing data prioritized before paid discovery.

The platform also made it possible to identify bottlenecks and quality issues that had previously been difficult to measure consistently.

What I learned

This project reinforced three principles that now shape how I approach engineering business systems.

1. Redesign the workflow, don't just automate it.

Replacing every spreadsheet action with an automated equivalent would have preserved many of the original problems. A shared data model created opportunities that separate spreadsheets couldn't easily support.

2. Good systems compound in value.

Every creator discovered, contact detail retrieved, and decision recorded can improve the usefulness of the platform for future campaigns.

3. Technical complexity should not become user complexity.

A system can be sophisticated behind the scenes while remaining straightforward for the people who depend on it.

My role and technology

My role: Sole end-to-end builder, responsible for product architecture, database design, frontend and backend development, integrations, automation, and deployment.

Development approach: AI-assisted engineering, with human-led product and architectural decisions.

Technology: TypeScript, SQL, Supabase/PostgreSQL, external APIs, Apify, and AI-assisted workflows.

The takeaway

I didn't set out simply to build a creator management tool.

I set out to solve a scaling problem inside an influencer marketing agency.

The result was a platform that made creator information reusable, consolidated operations, introduced measurable workflows, and gave the team a single system to work from.
